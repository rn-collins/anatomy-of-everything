# The Anatomy of… — series idea

Captured September 23, 2026 for RN's visual and media format collection.

## Reference

RN shared a screenshot of Jeroen van der Most's post about Luna Lu's artwork *Anatomy of a Hug*: two anatomical rib cages face each other, with their hearts close together. The post uses a scientific view of the body to ask a human question about closeness and what matters. Reference image: `Screenshot 2026-09-23 at 5.30.49 AM.png` in Polymath. Artist and title are attributed as stated in the shared post; independently verify credits before any published discussion of the artwork.

## RN's correction to the premise

“There's anatomy in everything”: for example, Formula 1 drivers train their necks for the forces of racing, and their low, reclined position inside the car is part of the design story. This series should move freely among bodies, sports, built environments, objects, technologies, performances, relationships, and institutions. **Anatomy** is the way in: reveal the structures, forces, positions, adaptations, and decisions behind something we think we already understand.

RN's recurring title pattern is **“The Anatomy of…”** followed by a specific subject: **“The Anatomy of… a Hug”** or **“The Anatomy of… an F1 Driver’s Neck.”** “There’s anatomy in everything” describes the editorial reach, rather than replacing that title pattern. The subject can be a human moment, a body part under an unusual demand, a movement, an object, or a system.

Choose the noun that the visual actually reveals. **“An F1 Driver”** is broad enough for a full profile of what the body does in the car. **“An F1 Driver’s Neck”** makes a sharper episode about resisting cornering and braking loads; the low, reclined seating position belongs in that story as part of how the driver is arranged within the car. If the visual gives equal weight to neck, seat, pedals, and machine, **“An F1 Driver in a Corner”** is the better subject.

The format naturally connects RN's anatomy and neurobiology training with design, culture, law, technology, and everyday life. Each story follows the subject rather than forcing every discipline into every post.

## Repeatable structure

1. **Subject:** Start with a visible action, object, place, or experience that makes someone ask, “Wait, how does that work?”
2. **Hidden anatomy:** Find the underlying body mechanics, design geometry, material, physical force, sequence of decisions, or institutional structure.
3. **Image:** Find the strongest existing primary-source photograph, artwork, artifact, or footage from its original publisher or rights holder. Inspect what it actually shows, confirm the *exact asset's* terms for the intended social post, and preserve the creator's credit. The first view should work before anyone reads a paragraph.
4. **Evidence:** Source the specific mechanism and keep literal anatomy distinct from metaphor or analogy.
5. **Consequence:** Show what the structure enables, constrains, protects, costs, or changes for a person. Some episodes end with awe or surprise; they do not all need a moral question.

The **F1 driver** is the strongest first test of the wider concept: the familiar broadcast image hides a trained body fitted into a highly constrained machine. Formula 1 describes targeted neck training to withstand racing loads and explains how a more reclined posture, higher pedals, and lower head became part of cockpit design. Do not collapse those into one causal claim: neck training addresses loads on the driver; seating geometry also reflects packaging and aerodynamic choices. Sources: [F1 driver training](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/neck-workouts-cardio-sessions-and-mental-exercises-how-to-train-like-an-f1.6c5HZRQvQv2MALjFF295Aq.6c5HZRQvQv2MALjFF295Aq); [F1 on the evolution of seating position](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/an-innovator-and-trendsetter-adrian-neweys-greatest-f1-contributions.3tFYlbhEcckb7cJAwKFTuy).

## Platform treatment

- **Single image:** A sourced visual carries the hook; the caption supplies the surprising mechanism and its consequence.
- **Carousel:** Start with the familiar view, reveal the hidden layer, explain the mechanism, then show why it matters.
- **Short motion:** Begin with the action or object, reveal its underlying layers gradually, and end by returning to the original view with new understanding.
- **Site story:** Show the visual, full source notes, image credits, accessibility text, and any distinction between literal anatomy and metaphor.

## Standing media rule from RN

Before proposing any image as a slide asset, identify its exact source file, visually verify its subject, and confirm the actual license terms cover a branded Instagram/LinkedIn carousel, commercial reuse if relevant, cropping/overlays, and the proposed attribution. Record the creator, source link, license link, and change notice. A link or tasteful credit alone is not permission. If an image fails this check, find a licensed alternative and adjust the story now; do not leave a blocked image as a future production step. Every slide gets a distinct, tailored original source image. Use primary existing imagery; do not generate images for this series.
